01 · SM 6610 — Knight v. Jewett Case Brief
SM 6610 | Sport and the Law
Week 2 Case Brief Template

Case: Knight v. Jewett, 3 Cal. 4th 296 (1992)

Student Name: Peyton Sullivan

Instructions

Read the assigned court opinion and complete each section below in your own words. Aim for approximately 1–2 pages of analysis, excluding this instruction text if retained. Use specific facts and reasoning from the opinion, not merely a summary of the outcome. Maintain Times New Roman, 12-point font, and double spacing for your responses.

1. Relevant Facts

Identify the parties, the incident, the alleged wrongdoing, and the procedural context. Include only facts material to the legal dispute.

Kendra Knight and Michael Jewett, A super bowl party in 1987, they were playing a halftime game of touch football. Knight had told Jewett to play less rough or she would stop playing. On the very next play, Jewett ran into Knight, knocked her down, and stepped on her hand. After three failed operations Knight had her little finger amputated. She sued for negligence and assault and battery. The parties disputed the details of the play. Knight and a teammate said Jewett ran into her from behind. Jewett said he jumped for an interception and landed on her. Jewett moved for summary judgment, arguing that by playing she had implied that there will be a risk of injury. The trial court granted it, and the Court of Appeal affirmed. The Courts of Appeal were split on how assumption of risk survives after Li v. Yellow Cab (1975), which adopted comparative fault, so the California Supreme Court took the case.

2. Legal Issue

State the legal question the court was asked to decide, preferably as a question.

How does the assumption of risk doctrine work now that California uses comparative fault instead of the all-or-nothing contributory negligence rule?

3. Court’s Holding and Reasoning

Explain the outcome and why the court reached it. Identify the rule governing participants in active sports.

The court affirmed summary judgement for Jewett. Reasoning being 1. Two types of assumption risk. Primary being the defendant owes no legal duty to protect the plaintiff from a particular risk. The plaintiff is completely barred from recovery. Secondary is the defendant did owe a duty and breached it, but the plaintiff knowingly encountered the risk. This came to a comparative fault, so the jury apportions responsibility rather than barring recovery. 2. Rejection of the “reasonable v. unreasonable” approach. Some Courts of Appeal had distinguished based on whether the plaintiff acted reasonably in encountering the risk. The court said this misreads Li. The real distinction is whether the defendant owed a duty. 3. Rejection of “implied consent”. The dissent argued that plaintiffs implied consent to known risks. The court called this a legal fiction. It would also make liability depend on each plaintiff’s subjective knowledge and would make summary judgement rarely available. The rule for participants in sports, defendants generally have no duty to eliminate risks inherent in the sport. They do have to duty not to increase those risks. For coparticipants, the court adopted the majority rule: a participant breaches a duty only if they intentionally injure another player or act so recklessly as to be totally outside the range of ordinary activity involved in the sport.

4. Negligence Elements

Discuss duty, breach, causation, and damages as they relate to the allegations. Which element was decisive, and why?

Causation and damages weren’t really in question. Jewett’s collision with Knight and stepping on her hand caused her hand injury and ended up losing her finger. Duty and breach were the big ones. The court said that in an active sport, participants only owe each other a limited duty: don’t intentionally hurt someone, and don’t act so recklessly that it’s totally outside normal range of play. Even if Knight’s version is true, Jewett was at most careless, so he didn’t breach any duty. Duty was the decisive element. It’s a legal question for the judge, not the jury, so the disagreements about exactly what happened on the play didn’t matter and summary judgment was proper.

5. Assumption of Risk

Explain how primary assumption of risk affects the duty analysis in this case. Distinguish inherent risks from conduct that increases those risks.

This case was primary. The court didn’t ask whether Knight personally accepted the risk. It asked what duty a touch football player owes other players. Since careless contact is part of active sports, Jewett had no duty to avoid it, and comparative fault never came up. The court also separated inherent risks from conduct that increases them. A defendant doesn’t have to remove risks that come with the sport, but they can’t make those risks worse. Here, rough, careless play was an inherent risk of touch football, and Jewett’s conduct wasn’t reckless enough to count as increasing it. The court’s concern was that liability for ordinary carelessness would discourage people from playing hard and change the sport itself.

6. Sport Management Application

As the director of a recreational flag football league, recommend one specific risk management measure and explain how it responds to the legal issues in this case.

I’d require trained referees who strictly enforce the league’s no-contact rules and eject players for rough or reckless play. Under Knight, players owe each other a duty only to avoid intentional or reckless conduct.

Provide a citation to the court opinion and any additional sources consulted.

Team Identification and Fan Consumer Behavior in Women's Sport

Problem Statement and Research Question

Sport consumer behavior research has historically centered on fans of men's teams, leaving women's sport largely understudied. Team identification, the level of psychological connection an individual holds with a sports team (Wann et al., 2001) is among the strongest predictors of fan behavior, yet a review of 157 team identification journal articles found only three focused on women's sport (Clarke et al., 2024). This gap is especially consequential given the rapid growth of women's basketball: WNBA average viewership climbed from approximately 170,000 in 2017 to over 500,000 in 2023 (see Figure 2), and total women's basketball attendance rebounded sharply to roughly 1.8 million by 2023 after cratering during the COVID-19 pandemic (see Figure 3).

The rationale for focusing on women's sport is threefold. First, women's sport organizations operate with limited marketing budgets, making evidence-based strategy essential (Clarke et al., 2024). Second, fans of women's sport may be motivated by factors such as gender equality advocacy that alter the classic identification–behavior relationship (Delia, 2019, 2020). Third, emerging theory argues that team identification is a multidimensional construct encompassing collective, role, and personal identity layers (Chun & Sagas, 2022), a framework with particular relevance to women's sport fandom. The research question guiding this paper is: How does team identification influence consumer behaviors attendance, media consumption, and merchandise purchase among fans of women's sport, and how do these patterns compare to men's sport fans?

Literature Review

Defining Team Identification

Team identification is the degree to which a fan incorporates a sports team into their self-concept (Wann & Branscombe, 1993). Genchev et al. (2021) distinguish two components: emotional involvement (psychological attachment) and personal commitment (engagement in consumption behaviors that bond fan to team). Expressions like "We won" or "I am a Wings fan" capture how team identity fuses with personal identity.

Chun and Sagas (2022) argue that most research has treated fan identity too narrowly, reducing it to a single social-identity dimension. Their Integrated Fan Identity (IFI) framework proposes three interlocking layers: (1) collective identity, through which fans gain self-worth from group membership and rivalry with opposing fans; (2) role identity, through which fans enact and verify the "fan" role in daily social interactions; and (3) personal identity, through which fandom aligns with core individual values and life goals. Identity work, "forming, repairing, maintaining, strengthening, or revising one's self-meanings" (Alvesson & Willmott, 2002, as cited in Chun & Sagas, 2022, p. 47) is ongoing and dynamic, meaning fan identity can deepen or shift across life stages and cultural contexts. The IFI framework is particularly relevant to women's sport, where supporting gender equality functions as a personal-identity motive that can draw fans into a team's orbit even before strong collective identification develops.

Team Identification and Consumer Behavior

Team identification's practical value lies in its relationship to fan spending and engagement. Genchev et al. (2021) surveyed 300 self-identified fans and found that team identification positively predicted in-person attendance, media-based attendance, word-of-mouth communication, and merchandise purchase. They describe these behaviors as constituting an "expanded relationship" between fan and team that generates revenue and promotional value for the organization while strengthening the fan's self-image through team affiliation.

Clarke et al. (2024) replicated and extended these findings in women's sport. Surveying 515 fans of England's men's and women's national football teams, they found all three behavioral outcomes: media consumption, merchandise purchase, and match attendance, significantly and positively correlated with team identification for both groups. For women's team fans, identification correlated most strongly with merchandise purchase (r = 0.672), followed by media consumption (r = 0.561) and attendance (r = 0.367). Men's fans showed a more uniform pattern (~r = 0.63 for media and merchandise; r = 0.590 for attendance). Table 1 summarizes these correlations.

A notable finding is that highly identified women's team fans rated media consumption (M = 6.05) above in-person attendance (M = 5.85) the reverse of men's fans at every identification level. Clarke et al. (2024) attribute this to historically limited broadcast coverage of women's matches, making media engagement an act of advocacy: watching validates the league's legitimacy. As broadcast access expands evidenced by the viewership surge shown in Figure 2; attendance may increasingly close this gap.

Gender and Team Identification

Research on men's sport consistently finds male fans exhibit higher team identification than female fans (Genchev et al., 2021). Genchev et al. (2021) confirmed this in their Oklahoma sample (male M = 2.02 vs. female M = 2.40, lower = stronger identification; p = .001). The pattern reverses, however, in women's sport contexts. Clarke et al. (2024) found that women's team fans (M = 5.31) scored significantly higher in identification than men's team fans (M = 4.85, p < .05), and within the women's fan group there was no significant gender gap (male M = 5.23, female M = 5.34). Social Identity Theory suggests that identification runs higher in smaller, less mainstream groups whose members feel a stronger sense of distinctiveness (Goldman et al., 2016, as cited in Clarke et al., 2024) precisely the position of women's sport fanbases relative to their men's counterparts.

Fan Motivations and the Visual Growth of Women's Basketball

Clarke et al. (2024) used the Motivation Scale for Sport Consumption (MSSC) to identify what drives fans to support their teams. Physical Skill and Achievement were the top two motivators for highly identified fans of both the men's and women's teams directly refuting the industry assumption that women's sport is a "family outing" product. Family was the least motivating factor for highly identified fans of both teams.

This emphasis on athletic quality aligns with the visual attendance data. As Figure 3 shows, women's basketball attendance peaked near 2.6 million in 2001–2002 before a prolonged decline, a sharp COVID-19 contraction (≈ 600,000 in 2021), and a strong rebound to approximately 1.8 million by 2023. The rebound tracks the rise of high-profile star athletes, suggesting that Physical Skill and Achievement motivations are translating into renewed spectator demand. Figure 1 shows that attendance concentrates in California, Texas, Arizona, New York, and Washington D.C. markets with established WNBA franchises reinforcing that local team access is a key moderator of the identification–attendance relationship.

Analysis and Interpretation of Findings

The evidence converges on four key conclusions. First, team identification drives consumer behavior in women's sport just as it does in men's sport: higher identification predicts greater media consumption, merchandise purchase, and attendance (Clarke et al., 2024; Genchev et al., 2021). This validates investment in women's sport fan identity-building programs and confirms that the psychological mechanisms underlying fandom are not gender-specific.

Second, the identification–attendance correlation is markedly weaker for women's sport fans (r = 0.367 vs. 0.590). Factors beyond identification, ticket affordability, market access, and broadcast availability play a proportionally larger role in attendance decisions for women's sport. The concentration of attendance in WNBA market states (Figure 1) supports this: access moderates how effectively identification translates into ticket purchases.

Third, the IFI framework (Chun & Sagas, 2022) explains a pattern unique to women's sport: fans can be drawn in through personal-identity alignment with gender equity values (the Feminism motive, M = 5.47; Clarke et al., 2024) before strong collective identification develops. Organizations can leverage this gateway by pairing social-justice messaging with showcasing athletic excellence, the Physical Skill and Achievement motives dominant among highly identified fans to progressively deepen fan identity across all three IFI layers.

Fourth, the WNBA viewership trajectory (Figure 2, ~200% growth from 2017–2023) demonstrates that media consumption is not merely a byproduct of identification but a mechanism that builds it. As new fans follow star athletes on social media and streaming platforms, identification deepens, eventually driving merchandise purchases and live attendance. Women's sport organizations should therefore treat digital and broadcast media expansion as a primary fan-development investment, not a secondary marketing channel. Taken together, these findings answer the research question affirmatively: team identification does meaningfully predict women's sport consumer behavior, but organizations must address access, broadcast equity, and multi-layered identity pathways to maximize its effect.
